Bioinformatics - Computation and Molecular Biology

Introduction

This lab is an introduction to some of the web and computation based resources for learning and experimentation in molecular biology. It is not structured in the traditional hypothesis, experiment, results, conclusion approach. It is more a free exploration. However, you should still keep writing in your notebooks. Make notes of interesting sites you wish to visit again and of your observations during the course of the lab.

It is usually possible to visit the computer labs except when they are booked.

The Challenge

You have some DNA sequences

  • What are they?
  • Why are there more known sequences that are similar to sequence 1 than sequence 2?
  • If you translate them into protein, and then search, why are there even more similarities?

Topics

DNA sequence databases

There are millions of DNA sequences from many different species stored in a datafiles called GenBank. These are on computers at the NCBI, National Center for Biotechnology Information in Bethesda, Maryland, USA. They are freely available for searching and downloading from anywhere in the world using the WWW.

Look and see what information is available at the NCBI.

Connect to the main page and start looking.

The NCBI databases can be searched in many different ways.

Using Entrez, you may look for DNA, RNA or protein sequences in GenBank using any descriptions of the sequence. Find Entrez and try entering your favorite species and protein or gene name into the search panel.

If you have a DNA or protein sequence from your own work and you wish to know if there are similar sequences that have been studied, then you may use the basic local alignment search tool (BLAST) to search the databases for sequences similar to your own. Use those sequences isolated from cDNA libraries made in Clay's lab. Amphioxus clone 8 is from a library made from 36 hour embryonic amphioxus (a protochordate) mRNA. Zebrafish clones 7 and 2 are from a library made from 48 hour embryonic zebrafish mRNA. Open the datafiles and copy the text into the blast search panel (including the first >description line - this is the FASTA format). These sequences are translated into proteins, but you do not know the orientation or the reading frame, so do a BLASTX search for similar proteins in the 'nr' (non-redundant) protein database. (BlastX will perform a conceptual translation of the input DNA sequence in all 6 possible reading frames and compare each of them to the several hundred thousand different protein sequences.

Macromolecular Structures

Many different proteins, DNAs and RNAs have been studied by X-ray crystallography. As a result, the 3-dimensional structures have been determined of many of the macromolecules you have been introduced to in the course. You may look for and view these by downloading the atomic coordinates and free viewing software from the NCBI. Go to the Structure Database and search for your favorite macromolecule.

Try looking for double stranded DNA, a tRNA, and some proteins. Smaller proteins, such as insulin, will be easier to understand at first.

(The viewing software, Cn3D, is free at: the Cn3d download site)
